Chains: does the network play its action cards in the right order?

The question rl-v3 started from: a turn in Dominion is a chain of action cards. Village gives two more actions, Smithy draws three cards, Festival gives two actions and two coins, and Throne Room plays the next card twice. Played in the wrong order, a hand that could make $12 makes $3. Can one network, which answers one card at a time, learn this, or does it need a model that calculates chains?

The short answer

We measured it two ways. First an exam: on 14,243 real positions from engine kingdoms, how often does the network pick the card that gives the most money this turn? Then the real test: does fixing its mistakes win games? We put a turn planner next to the network. The planner is a small statistical model: it tries every option on many shuffles of the unseen cards and plays each one out to the end of the turn. Then we played it against the same network without the planner.

Result: the network makes some ordering mistakes (Throne Room is its weakest spot), but correcting them with the planner or with a hand-written chain rule did not win more games. The mistakes are rare in the decks it actually builds and cheap when they happen. So rl-v3 has no chain module. The effort went into training instead: more games, new inputs, a curriculum against the champion, and a card school for the cards it never tried.

The exam

Positions: action decisions with at least two options, taken from 600 games on engine kingdoms (2–3 villages or cantrips plus 2–3 draw cards or Throne Room). Half the games were played by the GA genome, half by rl-v1's champion, both against the GA genome. Judge: the turn planner with 128 shuffles of the unseen cards. Each option is played out to the end of the turn by the champion net and scored by the coins at the first buy (+$1 per extra Buy). A choice counts as right when it comes within $0.05 of the best. Regret: the dollars lost per decision against the best option.

Where it goes wrong

The champion's costliest misses on the exam: the hand, the number of actions left, what it played, what the planner would have played, and the expected coins of every option.

Does fixing it win games?

Head to head: the net with the planner (or the rule) against the same net without it, in both seats, with the same kingdoms and seeds. 50% means no difference. ± is one standard error.

Why the coins planner does not help: it sees one turn only. It never plays Chapel or Remodel, because trashing costs money this turn, and it ignores what a card does to the next shuffle. The planner that judges by the network's own win estimate after the turn has the opposite problem: that estimate is too noisy to tell two orderings apart. Search over the action phase therefore joins search over buys (az-v1) on the list of things that did not beat the plain network.
