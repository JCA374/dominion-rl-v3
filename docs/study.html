Study this project

For someone who wants to learn from this project rather than follow it: what to read in which order, the ideas you need, the words we use, how to run it yourself, and a few exercises.

  2. Chains: the exam table and the head-to-head tests. This is the core finding. 10 minutes.

Key concepts

Measure before you build

The project began by asking for a chain calculator. Before building one into the player, we measured whether the network needs it. The exam scores single decisions; the head-to-head test scores whole games. Here: chain_exam.py, plan_vs.py. What people get wrong: a visible mistake (91.6% right) is not the same as a costly one. Only the head-to-head games say whether fixing it pays.

Determinisation (planning with hidden cards)

You cannot see the order of your draw pile. The planner shuffles the unseen cards K times, plays each option on each shuffle, and averages. The same shuffles are used for every option ("common random numbers"), so the luck cancels out of the comparison. Here: rl_plan with de_hide. What people get wrong: judging by the network's own win estimate looks principled, but that estimate is noisier than the differences between two orderings.

Warm start and zero-weight widening

To add inputs to a trained network without changing how it plays, give them zero weights in the first layer (and zero optimiser state). Here: widen_ckpt in train.py. What people get wrong: restarting the optimiser. Adam's running averages are part of where training was.

Forced exploration (the card school)

A policy that never takes an action cannot learn its value. Forcing the opening buys gives it decks with the card, and not recording the forced buys keeps PPO on-policy for everything it does choose. Here: rl_forced_buy, SCHOOL. What people get wrong: recording forced moves as the policy's own. Their probabilities would be wrong, and PPO's ratio with them.

Policy gradient

Change the network so that the choices in games that went well become more likely. The "how well" is the advantage. Here: ppo_update. What people get wrong: it does not know which of a game's 40 decisions won it; only averaging over many games sorts good decisions from lucky ones, which is why it needs millions of games.

Advantage and GAE

The advantage of a decision is how much better things went than the network itself expected at that moment. GAE builds it from the network's own win estimates along the game (λ = 0.95). Here: gae() in rl/jobs.py. What people get wrong: with a bad win estimate the advantages are bad too; the value head matters even though it never makes a decision.

PPO's clip

One update may change the probability of a recorded choice by at most ±20%; beyond that the gradient stops. It keeps learning from noisy data from destroying a good policy in one step. Where: clip, max_kl in CFG; the "PPO step size" chart on Training.

Exploration by sampling, evaluation by argmax

In training the network draws its choices at random from its probabilities; in every measurement it takes its top choice. What people get wrong: comparing the training games' win rate (with exploration) with the evaluation (without).

A league of opponents

Opponents from several sources so the policy stays good against all of them, not just the latest. Here: stage 2 adds rl-v1's frozen champion as 25% of the games; stage 3 everything. What people get wrong: pure self-play can cycle (A beats B beats C beats A) without getting better.

Reproduce it

8-core laptop CPU (i5-8250U), 8 GB RAM, Linux, Python 3.14, gcc:

git clone --recursive https://github.com/JCA374/dominion-rl-v3 && cd dominion-rl-v3
python3 -m venv .venv && .venv/bin/pip install torch --index-url https://download.pytorch.org/whl/cpu
.venv/bin/pip install numpy pytest pillow matplotlib
.venv/bin/python -m pytest -q tests                    # builds rl.so; "20 passed"
.venv/bin/python experiments/chain_exam.py build       # 14,243 exam positions, ~10 min
.venv/bin/python experiments/chain_exam.py score       # the exam table, seconds
.venv/bin/python experiments/plan_vs.py opponents/rl_r2_2200.npy --k 32 --leaf 1 --engine   # ~4 min: 48%
.venv/bin/python experiments/card_audit.py opponents/rl_r2_2200.npy --name champion         # seconds
.venv/bin/python -u train.py --run mine                # from rl-v1's checkpoint; ~40 s per iteration

Training needs about 3 GB of free memory (3,000 games per iteration).

Exercises

  1. Easy: a harder exam. In chain_exam.py, lower TOL to 0 and rescore. How much of the "wrong" is near-ties?
  2. Medium: Throne Room. The champion is weakest with Throne Room in hand. Build an exam of Throne-only kingdoms (engine_kingdom), then check whether rl-v3's newer nets improve on it.
  3. Medium: a better judge. Add a third leaf to rl_leaf: coins plus the win estimate after the turn, with a weight. Does any mix make plan_vs.py score above 50%?
  4. Hard: does the card school pay? Train two short runs from the same checkpoint, with school at 0 and 0.15, and compare their card audits and their head-to-head score.
