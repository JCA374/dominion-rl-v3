Chains of action cards, and the best Dominion model yet

The fifth method of the Dominion project started from one question: can a network learn to play its action cards in the right order (Village before Smithy, Festival before the draw, what to Throne), or does it need a statistical model that calculates chains? We measured it and got a clear answer (see Chains). That answer decided the rest: rl-v3 takes the strongest network so far, rl-v1's champion, and goes on training it with new inputs, a curriculum aimed at beating that same champion, and a card school for the cards it never tried (Cards). Goal: at least 55% head to head against rl-v1's champion, measured on 1,000 fresh games, while staying at or above its 82.8% against the GA genome.

What this is

Dominion is a deck-building card game. Every turn you play action cards from your hand, then buy cards for your deck. The player with the most victory points when the game ends wins. Inside one turn the order matters. Take a hand of Festival, Festival, Smithy, Merchant and four victory cards, with one action left. Play Smithy first and the two Festivals are dead: on average you buy with $1. Play Festival first and you keep the actions to play everything: on average you buy with $23 (a real position from the exam).

The question, answered. We built a turn planner: for an action decision it plays every option on many shuffles of the unseen cards, to the end of the turn, and picks the best. On 14,243 positions from engine kingdoms, rl-v1's champion network picks the coin-best card 91.6% of the time and loses about $0.09 per decision. It is weakest with Throne Room in hand (76%). Then the test that matters: with the planner (or a hand-written chain rule) choosing its actions, the network did not win more games against itself without it (48–50%, over 800–2,000 games per test). The network learns chains well enough, and the mistakes that remain do not decide games. So rl-v3 has no chain module: the network plays alone, about 3 ms per game.

What rl-v3 changes instead. (1) It starts from rl-v1's last checkpoint, which was still improving when it was stopped (67% → 81% against the GA genome over 2,200 iterations). (2) It reads 115 extra helper inputs from rl-v2: the exact odds of what the next hand brings, and what one more copy of each card would change. They start at zero weight, so it begins by playing exactly like rl-v1. (3) A curriculum that ends with a quarter of its games against the frozen champion. (4) The card school: the card audit found the champion never buys Chapel and rarely buys Mine, Library, Moneylender, Council Room, Smithy, Village or Merchant, so in 15% of the games its first buys are forced to one of those. It learns to play them, and it decides for itself whether to buy them.

What came before. dominion-v1 (an evolved priority-list genome, "GA"), az-v1 (AlphaZero-style search over buys, 70% against the GA), rl-v1 (one network for every decision: imitation of the GA, then PPO; its champion scores 82.8% against the GA and 71.7% against az-v1) and rl-v2 (the same network from random weights, by self-play: ~74%).

Approach

 rl-v1 r2 checkpoint (iteration 2,219: weights + Adam state)
        │  + 115 helper inputs at zero weight (plays exactly as before)
        ▼
 ┌────────────────────────────────────────────┐   choices, probabilities,
 │ 3,000 games per iteration, the net SAMPLING  │   win estimates, results
 │ 1 home league: GA, bots, az-v1, past selves  │ ──────────────────────────┐
 │ 2 champion:   + rl-v1's champion 25%         │                           ▼
 │ 3 open league: + rl-v2, everything earlier   │      advantages (GAE) ─▶ PPO update
 │ card school (stages 2-3): 15% of games start │                           │
 │   with 2 forced copies of an untried card    │                           │
 └────────────────────────────────────────────┘                           │
        ▲                                                                  │
        └──────────────────────────── new net ◀────────────────────────────┘
   after every update: 400 games vs GA + 300 vs bots + 200 vs the champion (measured only)
   every 25 updates: goal check, 1,000 fresh games vs GA + 1,000 head to head vs the champion
   every 100: chain exam + card audit of the newest net

Real numbers (8-thread laptop CPU, no GPU): 180,771 weights (413 inputs → 256 → 256 → 34 choices + a win estimate), running in C inside the engine. One iteration (3,000 games + PPO update + 900 evaluation games) takes about 40 s. The turn planner costs ~75 µs per rollout, about 60 ms per game with 32 shuffles. It is cheap enough, but it brought no gain.
